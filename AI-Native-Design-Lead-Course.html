From Figma Designer to AI-Native Design Lead

A complete, practical, self-paced course

Version: October 2026. Tool features change fast. Concepts in this course are chosen because they are durable; tool-specific steps are flagged with 🔧 and should be re-checked against current docs.

Who this course is for

You are a UX/UI designer who is fluent in Figma. You are about to become (or have just become) a lead designer, and you will be expected to:

  1. Lead the design practice: quality, critique, process, mentoring.
  2. Lead AI tooling adoption for the design team, and influence how product and engineering use AI to make UI.
  3. Build and run a design system that works for people and for AI agents.

You do not need to know how to code. You will touch code in this course, gently and with an AI agent doing the typing, because a modern design lead needs to understand the material that their designs become.

A note on "best practice" in a fast-moving field

You asked to learn only what is actually best practice and likely to stay. This course separates the two kinds of knowledge:

  • Durable (learn deeply): how LLMs behave, verification habits, context engineering, design tokens and systems, real-user research, design–dev contracts, change leadership. These were true in 2024, are true now, and will very likely be true in 2028.
  • Volatile (learn just in time): specific tool UIs, menu names, pricing, which model is best this month. These are marked 🔧. Learn them when you need them, from the official docs.

When a tool changes, you'll still know why you're doing what you're doing. That understanding is what separates a lead from a tool operator.

Module 01: The Shift, what changed and what will last

Time: about 3 hours · Output: your personal AI baseline and a capstone choice

Learning objectives

By the end of this module you will be able to:

  • Describe, with evidence, how UX practitioners actually use AI today, not how marketing says they do.
  • Separate durable changes in design work from hype and churn.
  • Explain the new shape of the designer's role and of the lead designer's role.
  • Start an AI log, the habit that powers the rest of the course.

Why this matters for a lead

Your team, your PMs and your executives will all have strong opinions about AI. Some will push mandates ("use AI for everything"), others will resist ("it's all slop"). A lead's first job is to replace opinions with evidence: what AI actually does well in your context, what it costs, and what it risks. This module gives you the baseline.

1. What the evidence says (2026)

In October 2026, Nielsen Norman Group published a survey of 521 UX practitioners about AI at work (source). Key findings:

Finding Number
Have access to generative AI at work > 99%
Limited to an approved set of tools 59% (only 12% can use anything)
Use AI features inside design tools 65%
Use at least one agentic tool 66%
Agentic use at startups vs nonprofits 85% vs 36%
Designers using agentic AI 71%
Top barrier: output quality 54%
Second barrier: data security or privacy 50%
Report no major barriers 9%
Average sentiment (1–5) 3.57, mildly positive
Open-ended comments with negative sentiment 69% (mandates, unreliable output)
Expect their role to stay important or grow 65%

Also from the survey:

  • AI is used most for desk work (writing, coding, desk research) and least when real users are involved.
  • Designers and researchers felt less job-secure than PMs.
  • Leadership pressure was common. Lack of executive buy-in was the least chosen barrier (6%). The problem is often too much push, not too little.

What this means for you:

  1. AI is already normal. You're not early, and you're not late either, because quality and integration are still unsolved.
  2. Output quality and data privacy are the real blockers. A lead who solves those (through context, systems, review gates and policy) creates real value.
  3. Many people feel ambivalent. Change management (Module 13) is as important as tooling.

2. What changed, and what didn't

Things that have genuinely changed (durable)

  1. Producing artifacts is cheap. Screens, copy, variations, prototypes and even working code can be generated in minutes. The bottleneck has moved from making to deciding, evaluating and integrating.
  2. Interactive prototypes are cheap. Complex interactions that used to wait for engineering (filters, dashboards, conversational UI) can now be tested with real users early (NN/G).
  3. Everyone can make UI. PMs generate mockups and engineers' coding agents choose button labels and error states. Design decisions are being made by AI on behalf of non-designers (NN/G: UX-context design).
  4. The audience for design deliverables now includes machines. Personas and specs were written for humans. Now AI tools also "read" your design system, your rules and your research. Context has become a deliverable.
  5. Design and code are converging. With the Figma MCP server, agents can read your design (components, variables, layout) to write code. They can also write back into Figma using your components, or capture live UI from a browser into Figma layers (Figma docs).
  6. Design systems moved from nice to have to critical infrastructure. AI multiplies whatever system it's given. A clean system yields consistent output at speed, and a messy one yields inconsistent output at speed.

Things that have not changed (and matter more now)

  1. You are not the user. Real users still surprise you, and AI-simulated users are narrower than real ones (more in Module 04).
  2. Good design is judgment. Choosing which of 30 variants is right, and why, is the job.
  3. Usability fundamentals. Heuristics, accessibility, information architecture and content clarity still apply.
  4. Collaboration and trust. Products are still built by teams. Alignment, rationale and shared standards still decide success.
  5. Accountability. Someone human signs off. "The AI did it" is not an answer to a broken checkout or an inaccessible form.

Hype to be sceptical of

Claim Reality
"AI will replace designers." AI replaces some tasks. Demand for judgment, systems thinking and research is rising.
"Synthetic users replace research." They can echo broad conclusions but produce far less variety than real people (NN/G study). They're useful for pilot-testing study materials, not for replacing users.
"Prompt engineering is the key skill." Context engineering, meaning what the AI knows about your users, system and standards, matters more than clever prompts (NN/G).
"One tool does everything." Teams combine a general assistant, design-tool AI, a prototyping tool and a coding agent.
"Faster means better." Speed without verification creates rework. NN/G respondents said mandates and unreliable output "eat the gains".

3. The new shape of the role

The designer's work: from making to directing and judging

Think of a designer's time as split between four activities:

          BEFORE AI                         AI-NATIVE
  ┌───────────────────────┐       ┌───────────────────────┐
  │ Understanding  20%     │       │ Understanding  25%     │  ← research, framing
  │ Making         55%     │  →    │ Making         20%     │  ← AI drafts, you refine
  │ Evaluating     15%     │       │ Evaluating     30%     │  ← critique, testing, QA
  │ Systematising  10%     │       │ Systematising  25%     │  ← systems + context
  └───────────────────────┘       └───────────────────────┘

These proportions are illustrative, not measured, but the direction of the shift is consistent across sources.

The AI-native designer's core loop

 Frame → Generate → Evaluate → Refine → Validate (with users) → Systematise
   ↑                                                                │
   └──────────── what you learned goes back into context ───────────┘

The last step is the new one. What you learn becomes context (rules, components, research insights), so the next generation of output starts better.

The lead designer's expanded scope

As a lead in the AI era, you own (or co-own):

  1. Quality bar: what "good" means, and the review gates that protect it.
  2. Workflow: where AI fits in your team's process, and where it doesn't.
  3. Design system: the shared infrastructure for humans and agents.
  4. Context library: the research, standards and rules that steer every AI tool in the org.
  5. Tooling and policy: approved tools, data rules, disclosure.
  6. People: skills development, psychological safety, and fair expectations around speed.

4. The capability spectrum

You don't need to become an engineer. But you should know where you sit on this spectrum and where you want to be.

Level Description Typical tools
0 Doesn't use AI none
1 Uses chat for text tasks ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini
2 Uses AI inside the design tool Figma agent, Figma AI features
3 Builds interactive prototypes with AI Figma Make, v0, Lovable, Bolt
4 Directs coding agents with design context Claude Code, Cursor, Copilot with Figma MCP
5 Designs the system that steers everyone's AI Design system, rules, UX.md, Code Connect, policy

A design lead should aim for level 4 competence and level 5 ownership. You don't have to do level 4 work daily, but you must be able to do it, so that you can judge it and lead it.

Common mistakes at this stage

  • Tool-hopping. Trying every new tool weekly. Pick one per category and go deep.
  • Measuring nothing. "It feels faster" is not evidence. Start the AI log today.
  • All or nothing. Either refusing AI or using it for everything. The skill is selective use.
  • Skipping fundamentals. AI amplifies skill. Weak fundamentals plus AI gives confident bad work.

Module 02: How AI actually works (for designers)

Time: about 4 hours · Output: a one-page "AI mental model" cheat sheet in your own words

Learning objectives

  • Explain in plain language what a large language model (LLM) does, and why that explains both its strengths and its failures.
  • Understand tokens, context windows, non-determinism, hallucination, sycophancy and mode collapse, and what each means for design work.
  • Distinguish between a chatbot, an AI feature in a tool, an AI agent, and the plumbing that connects them (tools, MCP, skills).
  • Predict before you try it whether a task is a good fit for AI.

Why this matters for a lead

You'll be asked "Can AI do X?" constantly. Without a mental model, you'll answer from hype or fear. With one, you can say "Yes for drafts, no for final decisions, and here's the review step." That's the difference between a tool enthusiast and a trusted lead.

1. The core idea: prediction from patterns

An LLM is trained on enormous amounts of text (and, for multimodal models, images, audio and code) to do one thing extremely well: predict a plausible continuation of what it has been given.

Everything else (answering questions, writing code, critiquing a design) is built on top of that ability, plus additional training to make it helpful, follow instructions and avoid harm.

Three consequences that matter to designers:

  1. It produces the plausible, not the true. Usually plausible and true overlap. When they don't, you get confident errors. NN/G's explainer on how generative AI works is a good non-technical read.
  2. It gravitates to the average. Ask for "a search screen" and you get the most typical search screen in its training data. NN/G puts it this way: "It knows what software generally looks like. It doesn't know your users" (source). Context is how you move it off the average, which is the subject of Module 09.
  3. It has no memory of you by default. Each conversation starts fresh unless the product adds memory, files or connections. What it "knows" about your project is only what's in its current context.

2. Key concepts in plain language

Tokens

Models read and write in tokens, chunks of roughly ¾ of an English word. Pricing, speed and limits are measured in tokens. Images also consume tokens.

Design implication: long files, huge Figma frames and big screenshots can use up a lot of the model's capacity. That's why Figma's MCP rules suggest fetching a metadata map first, then only the nodes you need (Figma custom rules).

Context window

The context window is everything the model can "see" at once: your prompt, the conversation so far, attached files, tool results and system instructions. It's large in modern models (often hundreds of thousands of tokens) but not infinite, and quality can degrade when it's stuffed with irrelevant material.

Design implication: more context isn't always better. Relevant context is better. NN/G's lesson 4 on context libraries is to give agents only the context they need (source).

Non-determinism (temperature)

The same prompt can give different outputs, because models sample from probabilities. Some tools let you control randomness ("temperature"); most hide it.

Design implication:

  • Good for ideation: run it again for more variety.
  • Bad for consistency: two people asking for "a settings page" get two different settings pages. Systems and rules create consistency, not prompts.
  • For testing AI features, you must test the range of outputs, not one happy path.

Hallucination

The model states something false with confidence, such as an invented statistic, a non-existent API or a fake citation. It happens because it generates plausible text, not verified facts. Hallucination has been reduced but not eliminated, and NN/G explains why it's hard to remove.

Design implication: verify facts, numbers, quotes, citations and accessibility claims. When AI says "this passes WCAG AA", check it with a real contrast tool.

Sycophancy

Models are trained to be helpful and liked, so they tend to agree with you and praise your work (NN/G).

Design implication: "What do you think of my design?" invites flattery. Ask instead: "List the five most serious usability problems, ranked by severity, with the heuristic each violates." Module 03 covers critique prompts.

Mode collapse (narrowness)

Generated outputs cluster around a narrow set of typical answers. NN/G compared 120 synthetic respondents with 604 humans. Both pointed to the same broad conclusion, but the synthetic answers used a much narrower range of responses (source).

Design implication: AI is a weak substitute for real user diversity and for genuine creative divergence. Push for variety explicitly and still test with real people.

Multimodality

Modern models can take images, screenshots, PDFs and sometimes audio or video as input, and generate images. They can "see" your screen and critique it.

Design implication: screenshot critique, layout-to-code and moodboard generation all work. Image understanding is good but can miss fine details like 1px misalignments or subtle contrast issues. Use real tools for precision checks.

Knowledge cut-off

Models are trained up to a date. Without web access they may not know recent tools, guidelines or your competitors' latest releases.

Design implication: for anything current, use a tool with web search and check the sources.

3. From chatbots to agents: the building blocks

Concept What it is Design example
Chatbot / assistant You ask, it answers, inside one conversation. Ask Claude to draft error-message variants.
AI feature in a tool AI embedded in a product, with that product's context. Figma agent generating a variant using your connected library.
Tool use The model can call functions: search, read a file, run code. The assistant searches the web, then summarises.
Agent A model that plans and executes multi-step tasks using tools, checking its own progress. A coding agent reads your Figma frame, writes components, runs the app and fixes errors.
MCP (Model Context Protocol) An open standard for connecting AI apps to tools and data, "like a USB-C port" for AI. The Figma MCP server lets agents in Claude Code, Cursor, Copilot and others read and write Figma.
Skills Packaged instructions and resources an agent loads when relevant (often a folder with a SKILL.md that describes when to use it). Figma's figma-use skill helps agents write to the canvas correctly.
Rules / instruction files Project-level instructions an agent follows every time: AGENTS.md, CLAUDE.md, .cursor/rules, .github/copilot-instructions.md. "Always use components from /design-system; never hardcode colours."
Context library The collection of files, databases and connections an agent draws on (NN/G). Your DESIGN.md, UX.md, component docs and research insights.
RAG (retrieval) The system searches a knowledge base and adds relevant snippets to the context before answering. An internal "ask the design system" bot.

What makes an agent different

A chatbot gives you an answer. An agent pursues a goal:

Goal: "Implement this Figma frame using our design system"
  1. Read the design context (via MCP)
  2. Look at the screenshot
  3. Find matching components in the codebase
  4. Write the code
  5. Run it and see errors
  6. Fix the errors
  7. Compare with the screenshot
  8. Report back

Agents are powerful because they iterate, and risky because they act: they can change files, send things and spend money. That's why governance matters, meaning what an agent may do without approval (Module 13).

Why MCP matters to you

Before MCP, every AI tool needed a custom integration for every app. MCP standardises that, which is why a single Figma MCP server works with many clients, including Claude Code, Codex, Copilot, Cursor and VS Code (Figma's client list).

As a design lead, the Figma MCP server is the bridge between your design system and every coding agent in your company. What you put in Figma (component names, variables, descriptions, Code Connect mappings) directly becomes what agents see.

4. Will AI help with this task? A quick heuristic

Score the task on four questions:

Question Favourable for AI Unfavourable for AI
Is a good-enough answer common in the world? Typical patterns (login, settings) Novel domain, specialist workflows
Can I verify the output quickly? Yes: visual, testable, checkable No: hidden errors, slow to check
What's the cost of a mistake? Low: a draft or exploration High: legal, safety, money, accessibility
Do I have the context to give it? Yes: system, research, rules No: it would have to guess
  • Four favourable answers: delegate the draft and review it.
  • Mixed: use AI as an assistant, with heavy review.
  • Mostly unfavourable: do it yourself, and maybe use AI to critique your work afterwards.

Examples

  • Generate 10 microcopy variants for an empty state → ✅ delegate.
  • Summarise 12 interview transcripts → ⚠️ assist. Verify quotes and watch for over-focus on the wrong theme.
  • Decide the information architecture for a medical dosing tool → ❌ do it yourself, with domain experts and users.

Common mistakes

  • Treating AI output as fact. Verify claims, numbers and citations.
  • Asking for validation instead of critique. You'll get flattery.
  • Expecting consistency from prompts alone. Consistency comes from systems, rules and components.
  • Overloading context. Dumping every file in degrades quality. Curate.
  • Anthropomorphising. "It understands our users" is wrong. It has text about your users if you gave it some.

Module 03: Working with AI, prompting, critique and verification

Time: about 5 hours · Output: your personal prompt library and a verification protocol

Learning objectives

  • Write prompts using a reliable structure, not clever tricks.
  • Use AI as a critic, not just a generator, without getting flattered.
  • Run a diverge → converge loop with AI that produces genuine variety.
  • Apply a verification protocol proportional to risk.
  • Build a reusable prompt library for yourself and, later, your team.

Why this matters for a lead

You'll review a lot of AI-assisted work from your team, from PMs and from engineers. You need a shared vocabulary for good AI use ("did you verify that?", "what context did you give it?") and reusable assets (prompts, checklists) that raise the floor for everyone.

1. Prompting fundamentals that last

Prompting tricks come and go with each model generation. These fundamentals don't, because they're about communicating a task clearly, as you would to a capable new colleague.

The 6-part prompt structure

Part Question it answers Example
1. Role / stance Who should it act as? "Act as a senior UX writer for a B2B finance product."
2. Goal What outcome do I want? "Write empty-state copy for the Invoices list."
3. Context What does it need to know? "Users are finance admins; they arrive here after setup; most have no invoices yet; tone: calm, precise, no exclamation marks."
4. Constraints What are the rules and limits? "Headline ≤ 6 words, body ≤ 20 words, one primary CTA, use the term 'invoice' not 'bill'."
5. Output format What shape should the answer take? "Return a table: headline / body / CTA / rationale. Give 8 options."
6. Quality bar / process How should it think and check? "Make the options meaningfully different in approach. After listing, pick the best 2 and explain why."

You don't need all six every time. But when output disappoints, one of these six is usually missing, most often context or constraints.

Other durable habits

  • Show, don't just tell. One good example beats a paragraph of description. Paste an existing screen, copy sample or component.
  • Ask it to ask. "Before you start, ask me up to 5 questions that would most improve the result."
  • Iterate in the same thread. Refine with feedback ("make 3 bolder, drop the playful ones") instead of starting over.
  • Break big tasks down. Brief → structure → content → visual, not "design the whole app".
  • Name things precisely. "Use the Button/Primary component" beats "a nice button".
  • Dictate when it's faster. NN/G found power users casually dictate prompts and invest their effort in context instead (source).

2. Diverge → converge with AI

AI is excellent at divergence (volume and variety) if you push against its pull toward the average. You stay in charge of convergence: choosing, combining and justifying.

Forcing real divergence

Bad: "Give me 10 ideas for onboarding." You'll get 10 variations of the same carousel.

Better:

Give me 10 onboarding concepts for [product], each built on a DIFFERENT
underlying strategy. Use these lenses, one per concept:
1. Zero onboarding (learn by doing)
2. Progressive disclosure over the first week
3. Template-first
4. Concierge / human-assisted
5. Import-first (bring your data)
6. Goal-setting first
7. Social / team invite first
8. Interactive tutorial with real data
9. Contextual tips only
10. Something from a non-software domain (e.g., games, hospitality)
For each: one-sentence concept, who it's best for, biggest risk.

Other divergence levers

  • "Assume the opposite of our current approach."
  • "How would [Stripe / Duolingo / a hospital / an airline cockpit] solve this?"
  • "Give 5 options a conservative enterprise team would ship, and 5 a bold startup would."
  • Ask for extremes ("most minimal", "most guided") to map the solution space.

Converging, which is your job

  1. Kill fast. Cut obviously weak options and note why in one line each. Your reasons are your design judgment, and writing them down trains it.
  2. Cluster. Group the survivors by strategy.
  3. Evaluate against the brief. User goals, business goals, constraints, risks.
  4. Combine. The best concept is often a hybrid.
  5. Decide and document. One paragraph of rationale. This becomes context later.

3. AI as critic

Generation is the obvious use. Critique is the underrated one. A good critique prompt gives you a fast, tireless "second pair of eyes", provided you defend against sycophancy.

Critique prompt patterns

Heuristic evaluation

You are a strict senior UX reviewer. Evaluate the attached screen against
Nielsen's 10 usability heuristics. For each problem:
- Severity: critical / major / minor
- Heuristic violated
- Exact location on screen
- Why it matters for [user type] doing [task]
- Concrete fix
List at most 10, ranked by severity. No praise. If you're unsure, say so.

Persona walk-through (a cognitive-walkthrough style)

Walk through this flow as [specific user: e.g., "a first-time admin at a
50-person company who has 10 minutes between meetings"]. At each step say:
what they're trying to do, what they see, what they'd likely do next, and
where they might hesitate or fail.

Red team

Argue AGAINST this design decision as convincingly as possible: [decision].
What would a sceptical engineer, a sceptical PM, and a frustrated user say?

Consistency check

Compare these 4 screens. List every inconsistency in spacing, type styles,
component usage, terminology, and interaction patterns.

Accessibility pre-check

Review this screen for likely WCAG 2.2 AA issues: contrast, target size,
focus order, labels, reliance on colour, motion. Mark each as "likely" or
"needs tool verification".

Rules for using AI critique

  • Treat it as hypotheses, not verdicts. Some findings will be wrong, generic or irrelevant.
  • Triage: accept, reject (and say why), or "needs testing with users".
  • Precision checks need real tools. Measure contrast with a contrast checker and accessibility with a proper audit tool and manual testing.
  • Compare with your own review first. Do your critique, then ask AI. The gaps on both sides are where you learn.

4. Verification: a protocol proportional to risk

Not everything needs the same scrutiny. Use risk tiers:

Tier Examples Verification
Low: drafts, exploration Brainstorms, moodboards, first copy drafts Skim for sense.
Medium: inputs to decisions Research summaries, competitive scans, critique findings Spot-check claims and sources; verify 3 key facts; check that quotes exist in the source.
High: shipped or high-stakes Production copy, legal or financial text, accessibility claims, data visualisations, code Full review by a qualified human, tool-based checks, user testing where relevant, an explicit sign-off.

The "verify 3" habit: for any AI research or summary, pick the 3 claims your decision depends on most and check them against the primary source. If any are wrong, distrust the rest and redo it.

Red flags that need extra checking

  • Specific numbers, percentages or dates
  • Named sources, studies, quotes or URLs
  • "Best practice says..." with no source
  • Accessibility or legal compliance claims
  • Anything that conveniently confirms what you hoped

Prompts you reuse are assets. Store them, version them, share them.

Format for each entry

Inputs needed: [screenshot, brief, persona...]

  ...

  ...

Common mistakes

  • Prompting like a search engine: three keywords and no context.
  • Accepting the first output. The first answer is the most average one.
  • Long chats that drift. After many turns, models can lose earlier constraints. Restate key constraints or start fresh with a summary.
  • Asking for opinions instead of structured critique.
  • No verification on medium and high tiers.

Module 04: AI across the design process

Time: about 6 hours · Output: a research-informed capstone brief, plus your stage-by-stage AI workflow map

Learning objectives

  • Apply AI appropriately at each stage of the design process: discover, define, ideate, design, validate, deliver.
  • Know where AI is strong, where it's risky, and where it should not replace human work.
  • Use AI for research support without corrupting your research.
  • Use AI for content and UX writing, visual exploration, and documentation.
  • Produce a capstone brief using an AI-assisted but human-led process.

Why this matters for a lead

You'll define the team's workflow. A lead who knows exactly where AI helps (and where it hurts) at each stage can write guidance that speeds the team up without lowering quality, and can push back credibly on "just use AI for research".

The map: AI by stage

Stage Strong uses Use with care Don't replace
Discover Desk research, competitor scans, domain primers, interview guide drafts, transcription Synthesis of real research data, survey design Talking to and observing real users
Define Problem-statement drafts, assumption mapping, JTBD phrasing, "how might we" variants Prioritisation suggestions Deciding the problem worth solving
Ideate Divergent concepts, analogies, extreme options, flows, IA alternatives Choosing among concepts Your judgment and the team's choice
Design Layout variants, content and microcopy, placeholder data, imagery, icons, states Visual identity work, brand-defining choices Taste, coherence, system decisions
Validate Test-script drafts, pilot-testing study materials, note-taking, initial clustering, heuristic pre-checks Analysis and interpretation Usability testing with real users
Deliver Specs, annotations, documentation, release notes, code via agents (Module 10) Accessibility claims, production code Human sign-off

This matches how practitioners actually work. NN/G's 2026 survey found AI is used most for desk-based work and least for work with users (source).

Stage 1: Discover

Desk research

AI (ideally with web search) is very good at fast orientation: "What are the main regulatory constraints for X?", "How do the top 6 competitors handle onboarding?", "Explain the workflow of a warehouse dispatcher."

Do:

  • Ask for sources and open them.
  • Ask for uncertainty: "What are you least sure about?"
  • Use the output as a map of what to verify and whom to ask, not as findings.

Prompt: domain primer

I'm a product designer new to [domain]. Give me a primer:
1) key roles and their goals, 2) typical workflows, 3) jargon (glossary),
4) regulations/constraints, 5) common pain points reported publicly,
6) 10 questions I should ask domain experts.
Cite sources where possible and mark anything uncertain.

Planning research with AI

AI is a solid assistant for drafting interview guides, screeners and survey questions. It's also good at reviewing them for leading questions, double-barrelled questions and jargon.

Review this interview guide for: leading questions, double-barrelled
questions, jargon, missing probes, and questions that ask users to predict
future behaviour. Suggest rewrites.

Synthesis: powerful, but handle with care

AI can transcribe, summarise and cluster interview notes fast. The risks:

  • Over-focus or wrong focus. NN/G notes that AI given raw transcripts may "over-focus on the wrong takeaway" or lose important points in the noise (source).
  • Invented or merged quotes. Always trace quotes back to the transcript.
  • Flattened nuance. Minority but critical findings (such as an accessibility blocker for a few users) get averaged away.
  • Privacy. Transcripts often contain personal data. Use approved tools only, and anonymise.

A safe synthesis workflow

  1. You read or skim all the raw material first, or at least a substantial sample, and note your top impressions.
  2. AI produces a first-pass clustering with quotes and transcript references.
  3. You check the references, merge, split and rename themes.
  4. You ask AI: "What evidence contradicts theme X?" and "Which participants don't fit any theme?"
  5. You write the final insights. AI can help phrase them, but the claims are yours.

Synthetic users: what they are and aren't good for

"Synthetic users" are AI-generated stand-ins for research participants. NN/G's 2026 comparison (120 synthetic vs 604 human respondents) found they reached the same broad conclusion but with far less variety, plus methodological problems such as the platform rewording questions (source).

Reasonable uses Not acceptable as
Pilot-testing your interview guide or survey wording Evidence of what users need
Generating hypotheses to then test A replacement for usability testing
Rehearsing as an interviewer Grounds for a product decision on their own

As a lead, put this in your team's AI policy (Module 13).

Stage 2: Define

AI helps you articulate and stress-test problem framings.

  • Turn messy notes into 5 alternative problem statements, then pick one.
  • Generate assumption maps: "List assumptions behind this solution, rated by risk and evidence."
  • Write jobs to be done: "Rewrite these needs as JTBD statements: When [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [outcome]."
  • Pre-mortem: "It's 6 months after launch and this failed. Write the 8 most likely reasons."

Deciding which problem matters is a strategic, political and ethical choice. That stays with you and the team.

Stage 3: Ideate

Covered in depth in Module 03 (diverge → converge). Additional ideation uses:

  • User flows: "Draft 3 alternative flows for X: minimal steps, maximum guidance, and expert mode."
  • Information architecture: "Propose 3 navigation structures for these 40 features; for each, name the organising principle."
  • Analogies: "How do airlines, hospitals and games handle 'something went wrong, here's what to do next'?"
  • Sketch to wireframe: photograph a paper sketch and ask the Figma agent, Make or an AI prototyping tool to turn it into a wireframe for discussion.

Stage 4: Design

Content and UX writing (one of the highest-value uses)

AI is excellent for drafting and varying copy, once you give it a voice and a terminology.

  • Empty states, errors, confirmations, onboarding, tooltips, notifications
  • Terminology consistency: "Here's our glossary; flag any text that uses non-preferred terms."
  • Localisation drafts: first-pass translations, plus checks for text expansion (German often runs about 30% longer, so test your layouts). Use professional review for production.
  • Readability: "Rewrite at a plain-language level; keep all meaning; show the changes."

Prompt: error message system

Our voice: calm, direct, no blame, no exclamation marks, no jargon.
Our glossary: [paste]. Error message structure: what happened + why
(if useful) + what to do next.
Write messages for these 12 error cases: [list]. Return a table:
case / message / action label / notes. Flag any case where you'd need more
information to write a good message.

Visual exploration

  • Moodboards and direction: image generation for mood, illustration directions and photography style references. Watch out for licensing and brand rules, and don't ship generated imagery without checking your company's policy.
  • Layout variants: the Figma agent and Make can produce variants quickly. Use them for exploration, then rebuild or refine with your system.
  • Placeholder and realistic data: "Generate 30 realistic but fictional invoices with edge cases: long names, zero amounts, overdue, foreign currency." Realistic data makes reviews and tests far better.
  • States: "List every state this component needs: empty, loading, partial, error, success, disabled, long content, permission-denied..." AI is good at completeness checks.

Brand-defining work

Core identity, signature interactions, and the "feel" of a product are where AI's pull toward the average hurts most. Use it to explore, but the defining choices should be deliberate human decisions, captured afterwards as context (DESIGN.md, Module 08).

Stage 5: Validate

  • Test planning: draft tasks and scenarios, then check them for bias and leading wording.
  • Pilot-test the script with synthetic users or colleagues.
  • Note-taking and transcription during sessions, with participant consent and approved tools.
  • First-pass analysis: cluster observations. You interpret.
  • Heuristic pre-check before testing, to catch obvious issues cheaply so that tests find deeper ones.

Never replace testing with real users. The whole value of testing is in what you didn't predict, and AI predicts the predictable.

Stage 6: Deliver

  • Specs and annotations: AI drafts behaviour specs from your frames ("Describe all interactions and states for this component"). You correct them.
  • Documentation: component usage guidelines, do's and don'ts, changelogs.
  • Handoff becomes context: in Modules 08–10, handoff turns into machine-readable context and agent-built code.

Disclosure: when to say AI was involved

As AI-generated content increases, so do disclosure questions, and the EU's transparency obligations started applying in August 2026, as NN/G notes (source). NN/G's PACED framework helps decide when to disclose:

  • Policy: is it required by law, your organisation or the platform?
  • Audience: how does your audience view AI?
  • Context: how much does human contribution matter here?
  • Expectations: would people feel misled if they found out later?
  • Degree: how much did AI actually contribute?

This applies to user-facing content and to internal deliverables, such as a research report that was largely AI-synthesised.

Common mistakes

  • Letting AI do the research. It can support research, but it can't meet your users.
  • Untraceable synthesis. Insights with no link to evidence.
  • Shipping generic copy. No voice and no glossary give you generic output.
  • Generating visuals you can't legally use.
  • Skipping states. AI makes it easy to check for completeness, so use it.

Module 06: AI prototyping and testing with real users

Time: about 8 hours, spread across 2 weeks · Output: a tested, interactive capstone prototype (v2) and a findings report

Learning objectives

  • Decide when a prototype needs to be interactive and when static Figma is enough.

  • Follow a reliable six-step AI prototyping process.
  • Create realistic data and complete states for prototypes.
  • Run usability tests on AI prototypes and avoid the fidelity trap.

Why this matters for a lead

AI prototyping is the most visible change in design practice, and the most misunderstood. Stakeholders will see a polished AI prototype and ask, "Can we just ship this?" A lead needs a clear point of view on what prototypes are for, how they're validated, and where they stop.

1. Why interactive prototypes changed the game

Before AI, complex interactions (filters, dashboards, data tables, conversational UI, multi-step logic) were tedious to prototype. Teams tested a few happy-path screens and waited for engineering to see real behaviour.

NN/G's 2026 article Test Complex Interactions Earlier with AI Prototyping describes what changed:

  • Ramp: a senior designer used Cursor to prototype a redesign of the expense-policy editor, with AI chat-based editing. Participants tested a working prototype loaded with their own sanitised data. The team found an issue with how edits were tracked and displayed that they "couldn't have found... without an interactive prototype". It went from concept to demo in three weeks and influenced the roadmap.
  • Ramp (conversational AI): a prototype connected to a real agent, so participants could type anything, revealing how the agent handled unexpected requests. Static screens can't simulate non-deterministic behaviour.
  • Purdue University: a traffic-data filtering interface was first built as static Figma wireframes, then rebuilt with v0 and Bolt using a synthetic dataset. With static screens, participants gave less specific feedback. With the interactive prototype, they identified specific problems.

The durable lesson: for complex interactions, realistic behaviour and data produce better feedback earlier. For simple questions (layout preference, content comprehension, visual hierarchy), static prototypes are still fine and often faster.

2. Static or interactive? Decide first

Your research question is about... Prototype type
Visual hierarchy, first impression, layout comprehension Static Figma (maybe a click-through)
Content and copy comprehension Static, or plain text
Navigation and IA findability Click-through or tree test
Filtering, sorting, search behaviour Interactive, with realistic data

Dashboards and data exploration Interactive, with realistic data

Rule of thumb: prototype the minimum fidelity that answers the research question. AI makes high fidelity cheap to create, but high fidelity can still cost you, through slower iteration, attachment, and stakeholders assuming it's done.

4. The six-step AI prototyping process

From NN/G's article, with practical additions:

Step 1: Decide what the prototype must do

Write the research questions and derive the required behaviour:

Research question: Can admins find and fix a misconfigured spending rule
in under 2 minutes?
Must work: rule list with search; rule detail; edit; validation errors;
save; undo. Data: 40 realistic rules incl. 3 misconfigured.
Doesn't need to work: login, settings, other modules.

Step 2: Make your design decisions before prompting

Layout, hierarchy, interactions and edge cases (empty, error, loading). NN/G: "The quality of the prompt... depends on the thinking you do in this step." Sketch or wireframe in Figma first.

If you leave design decisions to the AI tool, it will fill in the gaps, with average choices. The decisions remain yours.

Step 3: Collect or create data

  • Real data: only if allowed. Remove personal data, follow company policy, and check the tool's data policy.
  • Synthetic data: have an AI generate it from your criteria. Include edge cases: long names, empty values, extreme numbers, unicode, many items, zero items.

Prompt: realistic dataset

Generate a JSON dataset of 40 spending rules for a mid-size company.
Fields: id, name, category, limit (EUR), approver role, status
(active/paused/misconfigured), lastEdited, editor name.
Include: 3 misconfigured rules (explain why in a "notes" field), 2 with
very long names (60+ chars), 1 with limit 0, varied categories, realistic
European names. Return valid JSON only.

Step 4: Write a structured prompt (markdown helps)

# Prototype: Spending rules editor

## Goal
Test whether admins can find and fix misconfigured rules quickly.

## Users
Finance admins at 50–500 person companies; expert users; use it weekly.

## Layout
- Left: searchable, filterable rule list (status chips, category filter)
- Right: rule detail panel with inline editing
- Top: summary bar (total rules, # misconfigured – clickable filter)

## Interactions
- Search filters list as you type
- Clicking a rule opens detail; edits validate on blur
- Save shows toast with Undo (10s)
- Misconfigured rules show inline warning with "Fix" suggestion

## States
Empty search result; loading (300ms simulated); validation error; save
error (simulate on rule id 13); success.

## Visual
Use these tokens: [paste key tokens or DESIGN.md excerpt]. Dense, calm,
professional; no gradients; 8px spacing grid.

## Data
Use attached rules.json.

Step 5: Generate in the tool

Attach the prompt, data, sketches, screenshots and (if supported) your DESIGN.md.

Step 6: Edit, iterate, then pilot-test

  • Expect several rounds of iteration. Fix one thing at a time.
  • Pilot test with a colleague before real sessions, to catch broken flows.
  • Check that it works on the device and browser participants will use.

5. Getting AI prototypes to look like your product

Prompt-to-app tools default to a generic aesthetic. To pull them toward your system:

  1. Give exact tokens: colours, type scale, spacing, radius, ideally as a DESIGN.md (Module 08).
  2. Attach screenshots of 2–3 real screens as visual reference.
  3. Name components and patterns: "use a data table with sticky header", "use toast for confirmations".
  4. State what not to do: "no gradients, no emoji, no card shadows".
  5. For the highest fidelity, use a coding agent with your real component library (Module 10).

6. Testing AI prototypes with real users

The research method is the same as always. The new considerations are:

  • Moderated sessions work best for complex prototypes, because you can recover if something breaks.
  • Share a hosted link. Most builders provide one. Test the link from an incognito window first.
  • Reset state between participants (reload, clear local storage), or pre-seed data per participant.
  • For AI or conversational features, expect different outputs per session. Log what the system said in each session, since findings depend on it.
  • Tell participants it's a prototype. Some features may be missing.

Sample size: 5 participants per round finds most major usability problems in qualitative testing. Iterate, then test again.

Analysing: apply the safe synthesis workflow from Module 04.

7. The fidelity trap: prototype ≠ product

NN/G warns: a polished AI prototype prompts "can we just ship this?", but it may contain wrong patterns, confusing hierarchy and repeated elements, plus (from engineering's perspective) no security, error handling, accessibility, performance, tests or architecture.

As a lead, establish this rule: A prototype is a learning artifact. Production is built through the agreed pipeline: system components, code review, accessibility and QA gates (Module 11).

How to communicate it:

  • Label prototype links clearly: "PROTOTYPE – for research only".
  • In reviews, present what you learned, not just the prototype.
  • Offer the path to production: "Here's the validated design; engineering will build it with system components, and Make or MCP resources can speed that up."

Common mistakes

  • Prompting before deciding. You get the AI's design, not yours.
  • Toy data. "Lorem ipsum" and three perfect rows hide real problems.
  • Over-building. Prototyping the whole app when one interaction was the question.
  • Endless polishing instead of testing.
  • Shipping the prototype (the fidelity trap).
  • Skipping the pilot, so session 1 is wasted on bugs.

2. Design tokens

What a token is

A design token is a named design decision stored as data: color.bg.surface = #FFFFFF. Tokens are the single source of truth shared between design tools and code.

The three tiers

Tier Also called Purpose Example
Primitive Global, core, base The raw palette: every available value blue.600 = #2563EB, space.4 = 16px
Semantic Alias, system Meaning and intent, referencing primitives color.action.primary = {blue.600}, space.inset.md = {space.4}
Component Specific Decisions for one component (use sparingly) button.primary.bg = {color.action.primary}

Why tiers matter:

  • Theming: dark mode means pointing semantic tokens at different primitives, without touching components.
  • Rebranding: change primitives or aliases, and everything updates.
  • AI clarity: semantic names tell agents when to use a value. color.text.danger is self-explanatory; red.500 is not.

Rule: components should use semantic tokens and almost never primitives directly.

Naming tokens

A common structure is category → property → role/variant → state:

color / bg / surface
color / bg / surface-raised
color / text / primary
color / text / muted
color / border / default
color / action / primary / default
color / action / primary / hover
color / feedback / danger / bg
space / inset / sm | md | lg
space / stack / sm | md | lg
radius / sm | md | lg | full
font / size / body | heading-sm | heading-lg

Good names are predictable (someone can guess a token name they've never seen), purposeful (they describe intent, not appearance), and consistent (one pattern everywhere).

The W3C Design Tokens standard (DTCG)

The Design Tokens Community Group (DTCG) at the W3C published the first stable version (2025.10) of the Design Tokens specification (designtokens.org). It defines a vendor-neutral JSON format for exchanging tokens between tools. Figma, Penpot, Sketch, Framer, Style Dictionary, Tokens Studio, Supernova, zeroheight and others are listed as adopters or participants.

Conceptually, a DTCG token file looks like this:

{
  "color": {
    "blue": {
      "600": { "$type": "color", "$value": "#2563EB" }
    },
    "action": {
      "primary": {
        "$type": "color",
        "$value": "{color.blue.600}",
        "$description": "Primary interactive elements: main CTA, links, selected states."
      }
    }
  }
}

Key ideas: $value, $type, $description, groups (nesting) and aliases ({color.blue.600}).

Why a lead should care: a standard format means tokens can flow Figma → code → other tools without hand translation, typically via a pipeline (for example Figma variables → export → Style Dictionary → CSS variables and platform files). You don't need to build the pipeline yourself, but you should insist on one source of truth and know that the standard exists. 🔧 Check your tooling's current DTCG support.

Note $description: descriptions travel with tokens and are read by tools and agents. Writing them is a cheap, high-value AI-readiness step (Module 08).

3. Tokens in Figma: variables

🔧 Figma implements tokens as variables, organised into collections with modes.

Concept What it is Example
Variable A named value (colour, number, string, boolean) color/bg/surface
Collection A group of variables, often one per tier or category Primitives, Semantic, Spacing
Mode Alternate values for the same variables Light / Dark; Compact / Comfortable; Brand A / Brand B
Alias A variable that references another color/action/primary → blue/600
Scoping Limit where a variable can be applied Text colours only appear in text fill pickers

Recommended setup for this course

Collection: Primitives        (no modes; hidden from publishing if possible)
  blue/50…900, grey/50…900, red/…, green/…, amber/…
  space/0,1,2,3,4,6,8,12,16    (4px base)
  radius/0,2,4,8,12,full

Collection: Semantic color    (modes: Light, Dark)
  color/bg/*, color/text/*, color/border/*, color/action/*, color/feedback/*

Collection: Semantic size     (modes: Comfortable, Compact) – optional
  space/inset/*, space/stack/*, space/inline/*

Use scoping so designers (and agents) only see appropriate variables in each picker. That's a quiet but powerful quality guardrail.

4. Components

Anatomy of a good component

Aspect Good practice
Name Button, not btn-final-v3. Use slash grouping only for real hierarchy (Form/Input).
Properties Use component properties (variant, boolean, text, instance swap) rather than an explosion of variants.
Variants Only for real visual or structural differences: Variant=Primary|Secondary|Tertiary, Size=Sm|Md|Lg, State=Default|Hover|Focus|Disabled.
Auto layout Everywhere. Content-driven sizing, padding bound to spacing variables.
Bindings Fills, strokes, radius, spacing and type bound to variables. No raw hex values.
Layer names Semantic (label, icon-leading), not Frame 12.
Description When to use, when not to, related components (Module 08).
States Include focus (visible!), disabled, error and loading where relevant.

Match property names to code (critical for AI)

If engineering's Button takes variant="primary" | "secondary" and size="sm" | "md" | "lg", name Figma properties the same way. That makes Code Connect mapping trivial (Module 08) and makes MCP output closer to real code.

Tip: sit with an engineer and compare component APIs to Figma properties. Every mismatch is a translation error waiting to happen, for humans and agents alike.

Core component set for your capstone (6–8)

  1. Button: variants, sizes, icon options, states including focus and loading
  2. Text input: label, helper, error, disabled, with an optional icon
  3. Select / dropdown
  4. Checkbox / toggle
  5. Card / container
  6. Modal / dialog
  7. Toast / inline alert: info, success, warning, danger
  8. Empty state: illustration or icon, title, body, action

Then one pattern relevant to your capstone, such as a filter bar, table row or form section.

5. Accessibility built into the system

Building accessibility into the system means every product (and every AI generation) inherits it.

  • Contrast: WCAG 2.2 AA requires 4.5:1 for normal text and 3:1 for large text and essential UI components and graphics. Check each semantic text and background pairing in every mode.
  • Focus states: visible, consistent, and tokenised (color/border/focus).
  • Target size: WCAG 2.2 AA's minimum target size is 24×24 CSS px (with exceptions). Many systems use 44×44 for touch.
  • Don't rely on colour alone: pair danger red with an icon or text.
  • Motion: respect reduced-motion preferences, and tokenise durations.
  • Content: labels are required, and placeholders are not labels.

Document these in component descriptions, because agents will read them (Module 08).

6. Documentation basics

At minimum, every component needs:

  1. Purpose: what it's for, in one line.
  2. When to use / when not to use, plus alternatives.
  3. Anatomy: named parts.
  4. Properties: what each does.
  5. Behaviour: interactions and states.
  6. Content guidelines: label length, tone.
  7. Accessibility notes.

Free reference: Figma's designsystems.com has guides on space, grids, iconography, typography and content strategy.

Common mistakes

  • Primitives used directly in components, which makes theming impossible.
  • Visual names (blue-button) instead of purpose names (action/primary).
  • Variant explosion: 400 variants where 4 properties would do.
  • Figma and code mismatch in names and props.
  • Missing states, especially focus and error.
  • No descriptions, so humans and agents guess.
  • Building everything at once. Start with the 20% of components used 80% of the time.

Why this matters for a lead

This is where design leadership multiplies. Without AI-readiness, agents improvise. With it, every agent (yours, your engineers', your PMs') builds with your components, your tokens and your rules. You stop policing output one screen at a time and start shaping it at the source.

1. What "machine-readable" means

A human can infer meaning from visual context, history and conversation. An agent can only use what's explicit and structured. A machine-readable design system has:

Property Human-only system Machine-readable system
Names Frame 23, btn copy 2 Button, icon-leading, color/text/muted
Values Raw hex and px Variables / tokens with aliases
Intent In designers' heads In descriptions: when to use, when not
Code link "Ask Sam which component" Code Connect mapping
Rules Tribal knowledge Rules files in the repo
Visual identity Brand PDF DESIGN.md (tokens plus rationale)
UX knowledge Research decks UX.md / context library (Module 09)

The test: could a capable new hire build a correct screen using only what's written down and structured? If yes, an agent probably can too.

2. Component descriptions

Figma components have a description field. It shows in the UI and is part of what tools and agents can read. Treat it like a short spec.

Template

PURPOSE: Primary action trigger for forms and dialogs.
USE WHEN: One main action per view/section (Save, Continue, Create).
DON'T USE: For navigation (use Link); for destructive actions (use
Button variant=danger); more than one primary per section.
PROPERTIES: variant (primary|secondary|tertiary|danger), size (sm|md|lg),
iconLeading, iconTrailing, loading, disabled.
CONTENT: Verb-first, ≤ 3 words, sentence case ("Save changes").
A11Y: Min target 24×24 (44×44 on touch). Visible focus ring
(color/border/focus). Loading state must keep label for screen readers.
CODE: <Button> in @acme/ui (see Code Connect).
RELATED: Link, IconButton, ButtonGroup.

Write these for all core components. It takes about 10 minutes each and is the highest-ROI AI-readiness task you can do. (The Figma agent can draft them from your component, and then you edit.)

Also describe variables: color/text/muted: "Secondary text: metadata, helper text, timestamps. Not for disabled text (use color/text/disabled). Min contrast 4.5:1 on bg/surface."

3. DESIGN.md: visual identity for coding agents

In April 2026, Google Labs open-sourced DESIGN.md, a format for describing a product's visual identity to coding agents (GitHub; NN/G's coverage). It's a draft (alpha) specification, but the idea is durable and easy to adopt.

Structure

A DESIGN.md combines two layers:

  1. Machine-readable tokens in YAML front matter (or YAML blocks): colours, typography, rounded (radii), spacing, components.
  2. Human-readable rationale in markdown: what the values are for and how to apply them, plus do's and don'ts.

"Tokens give agents exact values. Prose tells them why those values exist and how to apply them." (DESIGN.md README)

Example (abridged)

---
name: Acme Finance
colors:
  text-primary: "#111827"
  text-muted: "#4B5563"
  surface: "#FFFFFF"
  action-primary: "#2563EB"
  danger: "#B91C1C"
typography:
  heading-lg: { fontFamily: Inter, fontSize: 1.5rem, fontWeight: 600 }
  body-md: { fontFamily: Inter, fontSize: 1rem, lineHeight: 1.5 }
rounded:
  sm: 4px
  md: 8px
spacing:
  sm: 8px
  md: 16px
  lg: 24px
---

## Overview
Calm, precise, trustworthy. Dense layouts for expert users; whitespace is
used for grouping, not decoration.

## Colors
- **action-primary** is the only colour for primary actions and links.
  Never use it decoratively.
- **danger** only for destructive actions and errors; always pair with an
  icon or text.

## Typography
One family (Inter). Max three sizes per screen. Sentence case everywhere.

## Do / Don't
- Do: use tables for comparable data.
- Don't: use gradients, shadows deeper than 1 level, or emoji in UI.

Tooling

The project provides a CLI that lints a DESIGN.md (broken token references, WCAG contrast checks) and diffs two versions to detect regressions:

npx @google/design.md lint DESIGN.md
npx @google/design.md diff DESIGN.md DESIGN-v2.md

🔧 This tool is new. Check the repo for the current spec.

Where DESIGN.md fits

  • Next to the code, in the product's repository, where coding agents read it every time.
  • Attached to prompt-to-app tools (v0, Lovable, Make) to pull prototypes toward your brand (Module 06).
  • Generated from your Figma variables. Your Figma variables are the source of truth, and DESIGN.md is a published view of them. Keep them in sync, ideally automatically.

4. Code Connect: the strongest Figma ↔ code link

Code Connect links components in your design files to components in your codebase. It makes Dev Mode show real production snippets, and it gives the Figma MCP server direct references to your actual code, so agents use <Button variant="primary"> from your library instead of inventing a <button className="bg-blue-600…"> (Figma docs).

Two ways to set it up

Code Connect UI Code Connect CLI
Where Inside Figma, with GitHub integration In the code repo
Who Designers and DS teams, with engineering Engineers / design engineers
Strength Easy start; one-to-many mappings (React, SwiftUI, Compose, Vue...) Full control via template files

They can be used together. CLI-created connections appear in the UI but are edited in the CLI.

What a mapping looks like (template file, conceptually)

// url=https://www.figma.com/file/…/Button?node-id=123
import figma from 'figma'
const instance = figma.selectedInstance

export default {
  example: figma.code`
    <Button
      size={${instance.getEnum('Size', { Large: 'large', Medium: 'medium', Small: 'small' })}}
      disabled={${instance.getBoolean('Disabled')}}
    >
      ${instance.getString('Text Content')}
    </Button>
  `,
  imports: ['import { Button } from "components/Button"'],
  id: 'button',
}

Notice how it maps Figma properties to code props. This is why Module 07 insisted on matching names: mismatched names mean more mapping work and more mistakes.

There is also a figma-code-connect skill that helps coding agents write these templates from a component URL.

Plan notes (🔧)

On Professional plans you can have one active framework at a time. Organization and Enterprise plans can have multiple. This matters if you ship web and native.

Your role as lead

You probably won't write the templates. You will:

  • Prioritise which components to connect first (the most-used ones).
  • Align Figma properties with code props.
  • Own the decision that Code Connect is part of the "definition of done" for a system component.

5. Rules files: instructions agents always follow

Rules are project-level instructions an agent follows every time, "the unwritten rules of your codebase that an experienced developer would pass on to a new teammate" (Figma).

Where they live (🔧 varies by tool)

Tool Typical file
Many agents (open convention) AGENTS.md at repo root
Claude Code CLAUDE.md
Cursor .cursor/rules/*
GitHub Copilot .github/copilot-instructions.md (plus path-specific instruction files)

Many teams keep one canonical file (often AGENTS.md) and reference it from the others.

What to put in them: design-system rules

From Figma's recommended general rules (adapted):

## Design system rules
- IMPORTANT: Always use components from `packages/ui` when possible.
  Never create a new Button, Input, Modal, etc. if one exists.
- Never hardcode colours, spacing, radii or font sizes. Use tokens
  (CSS variables from `packages/tokens`). If a token is missing, stop
  and ask — do not invent one.
- Follow WCAG 2.2 AA: labels on all inputs, visible focus, contrast ≥ 4.5:1.
- Use sentence case for all UI text. Use terms from /docs/GLOSSARY.md.
- Read DESIGN.md before any UI work.

## Figma MCP flow (do not skip)
1. Run get_design_context for the exact node(s).
2. If too large, run get_metadata, then re-fetch only needed nodes.
3. Run get_screenshot for visual reference.
4. Only then implement. Treat MCP output (often React + Tailwind) as a
   description of design intent, not final code style — translate it into
   this project's components and tokens.
5. Validate against the screenshot before marking complete.

Figma also suggests asking the agent to generate rules by analysing the codebase (where tokens live, the component architecture, styling approach, icon system), then reviewing them. A full template is in templates/AGENTS.md.

Rules hygiene

  • Short and specific beats long and vague. Each rule should be testable.
  • Use "IMPORTANT" sparingly, for the rules that matter most.
  • Version them in git, review changes like code, and give them an owner (Module 13).
  • Update them from failures. When an agent drifts, ask which rule would have prevented it, then add it (the "drift log", Module 10).

6. Using AI to audit your design system

Agents can help maintain the system too.

  • Figma agent: "Find all layers in this file using raw colour values instead of variables" or "List detached instances".
  • Write-to-canvas agent (beta, use a duplicate!): "Convert the raw values in this section to variables."
  • Coding agent on the repo: "Find hardcoded hex colours and px values in /src that should be tokens. Produce a table with file, line, value and suggested token."
  • DESIGN.md lint: contrast and broken references.
  • Consistency: "Compare these Figma components' properties with the props of the matching React components. List mismatches."

7. The AI-readiness scorecard

Score each 0–2 (0 = absent, 1 = partial, 2 = solid):

# Criterion
1 All colours, spacing, radii and type use variables or styles (no raw values)
2 Token tiers exist (primitive → semantic), with semantic names
3 Variables have descriptions
4 Components have semantic names and layer names
5 Components have structured descriptions (purpose, use/don't, a11y, code)
6 Figma properties match code props
7 Code Connect covers the top 10 components
8 DESIGN.md exists, is linted and is in sync with Figma
9 Rules file exists, is versioned and owned
10 A context-comparison test has shown improvement (Module 09)

0–7: not AI-ready; agents will improvise. 8–14: partially ready; expect drift. 15–20: AI-ready; agents mostly stay on-system.

Common mistakes

  • Writing a rules novel. Agents follow concise, specific rules better.
  • DESIGN.md out of sync with Figma. Two sources of truth means no truth.
  • Connecting everything with Code Connect at once. Start with the top 10.
  • Running write-to-canvas audits on the production library.
  • No owner for the rules. They go stale (NN/G: context needs ongoing maintenance).

Module 09: Context engineering and UX-context design

Time: about 6 hours · Output: UX.md (your product's UX context), a context library structure, and a with/without-context experiment

Learning objectives

  • Explain context engineering and why it now matters more than prompt wording.
  • Apply NN/G's global / local / ambient model of context.
  • Practise UX-context design: turning research and standards into context that steers everyone's AI.
  • Write a UX.md covering research synthesis, interaction standards, glossary, user models and world models.
  • Run a context experiment to measure whether your context improves output.
  • Maintain a context library without building a "big ball of mud".

Why this matters for a lead

This is probably the most important new responsibility of an AI-era design lead. In many organisations, designers are no longer the only people producing designs: PMs make mockups and engineers' coding agents choose labels and error states. NN/G's answer is not gatekeeping. It is to make sure everything AI generates, no matter who prompts it, is informed by the organisation's knowledge of its users and design standards (source).

If you own that context, your influence extends to every screen, including the ones you never see.

1. From prompts to context

Context is everything the model can see when it does the work: your request plus instructions, standards, examples and background information (NN/G).

  • Prompt engineering: how you phrase the request.
  • Context engineering: what knowledge, rules and examples surround the request, and how they're curated, structured, scoped and maintained.

NN/G's research with AI power users found they spent more effort engineering context libraries than crafting prompts, and most prompts were casually dictated (source). "Curating the right context now matters even more than writing a good prompt" (source).

An analogy: the house builder who never meets your family designs the average house, which is wrong in ways that matter if you use a wheelchair or work from home. "That's not the builder's fault; the problem is the builder didn't have context." (NN/G)

2. The three roles of context

From NN/G's study of AI power users (source):

Role Definition Design examples
Global Rarely changes; shapes the agent's actions across all tasks Design principles, DESIGN.md, UX.md, accessibility rules, glossary, governance rules
Local Intentionally scoped to a specific task or project This feature's brief, research findings for this flow, the Figma frame, the test plan
Ambient Raw, uncurated streams the agent sifts through Slack threads, meeting transcripts, email, support tickets, analytics 
        ┌────────────── ambient (Slack, transcripts, tickets) ──────────────┐
        │                                                                    │
        │     [local: brief]   [local: research]   [local: Figma frame]      │
        │            \               |                    /                  │
        │             ───────→  AI OUTPUT  ←──────────────                   │
        │                           ↑                                        │
        │   ═══════ global: principles · DESIGN.md · UX.md · rules ═══════   │
        └────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

Information can change roles. A Slack decision (ambient) becomes local when you point the agent at it, and may become global if it turns into a standing rule.

Design-lead implication: your job is mostly to curate global context (the standards everyone shares) and to define templates for local context (what a good feature brief contains). Be cautious with ambient context: it's noisy and can contain sensitive data.

3. UX-context design

UX-context design is the practice of discovering and curating what an organization knows and wants into the context that guides everything its AI tools generate: from who its users are and the world they live in, to how a product should look and behave. (NN/G)

It also involves testing the context with the models your team uses and refining it.

Why traditional deliverables aren't enough

A persona has a stock photo and a name to help humans empathise. "A model does not need persuading, it needs the underlying reasoning." (NN/G) So AI-ready deliverables are:

  • Distilled: findings stated as clear, reasoned insights.
  • Curated by a skilled human: not raw transcripts dumped in.
  • Available to everyone who designs, regardless of role.
  • Living next to the product: in the repo or a shared library, read on every generation.
  • Measured by output quality, not by stakeholder persuasion.

Curation, not handoff

Traditional deliverables are handed off and left behind. UX context is continuously curated: "New research updates it, and so does watching what the AI gets wrong." (NN/G)

4. Writing UX.md

NN/G proposes UX.md as a hypothesis: a broader companion to DESIGN.md. DESIGN.md covers visual identity; UX.md covers experience knowledge. For large products, UX.md can be an index to a folder of context files.

Recommended sections

1. Research synthesis: insights as constraints

Write findings so they constrain generation:

❌ "Users find onboarding hard." ✅ "Users abandon setup when asked for information they don't have on hand. → Never require external info (IDs, documents) in the first session; allow save-and-return."

Include the evidence strength (for example "n=12 interviews, 2025-Q4") so agents and humans know how much to trust each one.

2. User models

Expertise, goals, concerns, frequency of use, what doesn't work for them. No stock photos, just reasoning:

"Finance admins: expert users, work in the product 2–4 h/day during month-end, prefer density and keyboard shortcuts, distrust automation that changes numbers without showing the change."

NN/G's example of how a user model leans generation: if users spend hours making complex decisions, lean toward denser screens. If they use it a few minutes a month, lean toward fewer choices per screen.

3. World models (context of use)

The conditions users are in: interruptions (a nurse on a hospital floor), stress (filing a claim after an accident), compliance (every action must leave an audit trail), devices, connectivity, environment.

4. Interaction standards

How the product behaves:

  • When to confirm versus allow undo
  • How errors are worded and placed
  • Expert versus novice optimisation
  • Loading, empty and partial states
  • Notifications: when, where, how loud

5. Glossary

The words users and domain experts use, with definitions and forbidden synonyms: "Users say 'case', not 'ticket'." NN/G notes a side effect: users' terms start appearing even in code, because agents pick them up.

6. Pointers

Links to DESIGN.md, the component library location, the accessibility standard and key research reports.

A full template is in templates/UX.md.

5. Building a context library

A context library is "a collection of institutional and procedural knowledge, contained within markdown files or external software, that AI agents may reference and edit" (NN/G).

Suggested structure for a product team

/context
  README.md               ← index: what's here, when to use what, owners
  UX.md                   ← global UX context (or index)
  DESIGN.md               ← global visual identity
  GLOSSARY.md             ← global terminology
  principles.md           ← design principles with do/don't
  accessibility.md        ← standards and checks
  /research
    2026-q3-onboarding.md ← distilled insights, evidence strength, date
  /features
    _brief-template.md    ← local-context template
    invoices-redesign.md  ← local context for an active project
  /skills                 ← packaged procedures (optional, tool-dependent)
AGENTS.md                 ← rules; points agents to /context

NN/G's four lessons (from power users)

  1. Context needs ongoing maintenance. Files written once and used for months go stale. Schedule reviews and audit context against recent decisions.
  2. Let the agent update context for you, then verify. Most power users asked the AI to edit context files rather than typing into them. It's faster and keeps the style consistent, but review the changes, because users "rarely checked what Claude had changed".
  3. Structure the library for findability. Clear names, an index, one topic per file.
  4. Give agents only the context they need. More isn't better: irrelevant context dilutes quality and wastes tokens.

Skills: packaging procedures

Skills package instructions (and sometimes scripts and resources) that an agent loads when relevant, typically a folder with a SKILL.md describing when to use it. Examples: "write UX copy using our voice", "run a heuristic review using our checklist", "create a feature brief". Figma ships skills for its MCP server (such as figma-use and figma-code-connect). As the team grows, turning frequent prompts into skills is the natural evolution from your prompt library (Module 03).

6. Testing context: the with/without experiment

UX-context design must be tested. NN/G lists open questions: which artifacts help most, when raw data helps, how to measure efficacy, and whether there is such a thing as too much context. Your team can answer these for your product.

Method

  1. Pick 3 representative generation tasks: for example "invoice list screen", "error state for failed payment", "onboarding step 2".
  2. Define a rubric (score 1–5): - Uses system components and tokens correctly - Fits users' expertise and context (density, terminology) - Follows interaction standards (confirm/undo, error style) - Accessibility basics - Copy matches voice and glossary
  3. Generate each task under 3 conditions: - A: prompt only - B: prompt + DESIGN.md - C: prompt + DESIGN.md + UX.md (+ rules)
  4. Run each condition 2–3 times (outputs vary).
  5. Score blind if possible: have a colleague score without knowing the condition.
  6. Analyse: which context moved which rubric line? What did the AI still get wrong, and which context would fix it?
  7. Update the context, then re-test. Keep a changelog.

This gives you evidence for leadership ("adding UX.md raised the average rubric score from 2.4 to 3.9") and tells you where to invest.

A template is in templates/context-experiment.md.

7. Avoid building a big ball of mud

NN/G found AI power users ("vibe architects") repeatedly building "big balls of mud": systems "scraped together for short-term functionality, not architected for long-term durability" (source). Because building feels free, people overbuild. The patterns they saw include:

  • Throwaway code becomes permanent: a quick rules.md draft that everything comes to depend on.
  • Piecemeal growth: features bolted on as needs arise, until nobody understands the whole.

…and others from Foote and Yoder's original catalogue, leading to systems that are hard to direct, diagnose or rebuild.

Guardrails for your context library

  • Plan the structure before writing lots of files (use the structure above).
  • One owner per file, and a review cadence (for example quarterly, or after each research round).
  • Changelog: what changed, why, and what triggered it.
  • Prune: delete or archive stale context. Context debt is real.
  • Prefer fewer, better files over many overlapping ones.
  • Re-test (section 6) after big changes.
  • Rebuild deliberately when the system gets too tangled, rather than patching forever.

8. Privacy and safety in context

  • No personal data in context files. Distil insights, don't paste transcripts.
  • No secrets (keys, credentials) in any file an agent reads.
  • Check where context goes: which tools read it, and what their data policies are.
  • Ambient connections (Slack, email, MCP to Notion and so on) can expose more than intended. Scope them deliberately. NN/G also notes connections break ("system decay"), so expect maintenance.

Common mistakes

  • Dumping raw research into context. NN/G warns of noise and over-focus on the wrong things.
  • Writing for humans: persuasive decks instead of reasoned constraints.
  • One giant file, or the opposite: 60 overlapping files.
  • Never testing whether the context helps.
  • Never updating it: write once, rot forever.
  • No owner.

Root causes usually fall into five types:

  1. Missing rule
  2. Missing or ambiguous context (DESIGN.md or UX.md gap)
  3. Design system gap (component or token doesn't exist)
  4. Figma hygiene issue (raw values, bad names, unmapped component)
  5. Model limitation (rare once 1–4 are solved)

As a lead, the drift log becomes your evidence base for system investment: "60% of drift came from missing Code Connect mappings."

  • [ ] Labels on inputs; meaningful button text; alt text where needed
  • [ ] Contrast checked in all modes

Module 11: Designer–developer collaboration, rebuilt

Time: about 4 hours · Output: a designer–developer collaboration contract, a RACI, and a definition of done for AI-assisted UI

Learning objectives

  • Describe how the dynamics between design and engineering change when both can generate UI.
  • Identify the common friction points and their causes.
  • Define ownership (RACI) for the system, context, prototypes and production code.
  • Design review gates and a shared definition of done.
  • Set up rituals that keep design and code in sync.

Why this matters for a lead

Handoff used to be a clear line: design in Figma, then engineering builds. Now designers ship coded prototypes, engineers' agents generate UI, PMs make mockups, and everyone's output looks "finished". Without explicit agreements, you get duplicated components, off-brand UI, turf wars and quality regressions. Defining this collaboration is a core lead responsibility.

1. What changed in the relationship

Before Now
Designers make pictures; engineers make software Both make working UI, at different fidelity and quality levels
Handoff via specs and redlines Handoff via context: system, Code Connect, rules, MCP
Design system as a Figma library plus a separate code library One system, with Figma and code linked (variables ↔ tokens, Code Connect)
Design reviews screenshots Design reviews running UI and pull requests
Engineers interpret designs Agents interpret designs, so rules and context decide accuracy
Designers are the only ones who design Everyone designs, so standards must be machine-readable to reach them

What each side gains

Designers:

  • Test realistic interactions early (Module 06).
  • Influence code directly through the system and rules.
  • See exactly what was built (code to canvas).

Engineers:

  • Faster first passes from accurate design context.
  • Fewer ambiguous specs, because tokens and components are explicit.
  • Less pixel-pushing, more architecture and quality work.

2. Common friction points (and what's really going on)

Friction Underlying cause Resolution
"Design's prototype code is unusable." The prototype was treated as production (fidelity trap) Prototypes are for learning; production goes through the pipeline
"Engineers' AI-generated UI is off-brand." Missing rules, context or Code Connect Ship AI-ready system assets (Modules 08–09)
"We have 4 different modals." No "use existing components" rule; no governance Rules plus a contribution model; "stop and list what's missing"
"Designers are touching our code!" Unclear ownership RACI: designers may prototype and propose; engineers own production
"Design keeps changing things after build." Late review, no shared definition of done Review gates earlier, on running UI
"The Figma file doesn't match production." No sync process Token pipeline, Code Connect, code-to-canvas reviews
"AI is just making us do more, faster." Mandates without workflow change Measure the real gains; protect quality time (Module 13)

3. Ownership: a RACI for AI-era UI

Code component library C I R A C I
Token pipeline (Figma ↔ code) C I R A R I
Code Connect mappings C I R A R I
DESIGN.md A C R C I I
UX.md / research context A R C I I C

Key principle: designers may contribute code (prototypes, proposals, even PRs to the component library), but engineering is accountable for production code, and design is accountable for the experience.

4. Review gates

Gates are where quality is protected. Keep them few, clear and early.

 Idea → [Gate 1] → Prototype & test → [Gate 2] → Build → [Gate 3] → Release
Gate When Who Checks
1. Direction review Before significant build or prototype Design lead, PM, eng lead Problem, evidence, approach, system fit
2. Design-ready review Before production build Design + eng Validated with users? States complete? System components identified? Gaps logged?
3. Design QA on running UI On the PR or staging Designer (+ a11y check) QA checklist (Module 10), Figma parity, accessibility, copy

Make Gate 3 lightweight but mandatory. Ideally designers review PR preview deployments (many teams auto-deploy each PR to a temporary URL) and comment directly on the PR.

5. Definition of done for AI-assisted UI

A UI change is "done" when:

  • [ ] Built from system components; any new component went through the contribution process
  • [ ] No hardcoded design values; tokens only
  • [ ] All designed states implemented
  • [ ] Accessibility: keyboard, focus, labels, contrast and screen-reader basics checked
  • [ ] Copy matches voice and glossary
  • [ ] Design QA passed (Gate 3)
  • [ ] Code reviewed by an engineer
  • [ ] If a new pattern emerged: Figma, code, Code Connect and docs all updated
  • [ ] If drift was found: drift log updated and a rule or context change proposed

7. The contribution model (how the system grows)

When a team needs something the system doesn't have:

  1. Check: does something existing work, perhaps with a new variant?
  2. Propose: a short request covering the need, use cases and a design proposal.
  3. Review: the DS team decides between a one-off, a new variant or a new component.
  4. Build both sides: Figma component, code component, Code Connect, docs and description.
  5. Announce: changelog and a rules or context update if needed.

Agents participate too: the "stop and list what's missing" rule (Module 10) feeds this process automatically.

8. Communication principles

  • Lead with shared goals: quality, speed and user outcomes, not ownership.
  • Talk about the system, not people: "the rule was missing" rather than "you did it wrong".
  • Make your work inspectable: decisions in docs, rationale in context files.
  • Learn their tools (Module 10). Credibility comes from understanding.
  • Invite engineers into design critique, and ask to join code reviews for UI.

Common mistakes

  • Designers shipping prototype code to production without engineering review.
  • Engineering ignoring design QA because "the AI matched Figma".
  • No contribution process, so the system fragments.
  • Treating AGENTS.md as engineering-only. Design-system rules belong to design too.
  • Gates too late, with review only after release.

Module 12: Designing AI-powered product features

Time: about 4 hours · Output: an AI-feature review of a real product, plus an AI feature concept for your capstone that passes the "value first" test

This course is mostly about using AI to design. But as a lead you'll also be asked to design AI features into products, and you'll review your team's AI feature work. This module gives you the durable principles.

Learning objectives

  • Decide whether AI adds value to a feature, before deciding how.
  • Recognise the main interaction paradigms for AI features (intent-based, chat, inline, generative UI, agents).
  • Design for non-determinism, errors and trust.
  • Apply guidelines for transparency, control, explanation and disclosure.
  • Design for AI agents as users of your product.

Why this matters for a lead

AI features are where hype hits users hardest: sparkle buttons nobody understands, chatbots that can't help, agents that act without consent. A lead protects users and the business by insisting on value first, then trustworthy design.

1. Value first: should this be AI at all?

NN/G is blunt: "Powered by AI" is not a value proposition, and AI features must solve real user problems (study guide). Novelty rarely produces value. Start from the problem, not the technology.

Where AI genuinely helps

NN/G's four AI "superpowers": content creation, summarisation, basic data analysis and perspective-taking (source). If your feature idea doesn't use at least one, question it.

The value-first test

  1. What user problem does this solve? Is there evidence it exists?
  2. What do users do today without AI? Is the AI way clearly better?
  3. Could a non-AI solution work (better defaults, search, a filter, a template)?
  4. What happens when the AI is wrong? Is that acceptable for this task?
  5. Can users tell what it does? (NN/G found users often can't tell what a site's AI chatbot is for.)
  6. Is the scope narrow enough? Narrowly scoped AI features are easier to understand and see better adoption (source).

2. Interaction paradigms

Intent-based interaction

NN/G describes AI as the first new UI paradigm in 60 years: intent-based outcome specification, where users state what they want rather than how to do it (source).

Its weakness is the articulation barrier: many users struggle to express what they want in words. The solution is hybrid interfaces, combining prompts with GUI controls (source).

Patterns and when to use them

Pattern Good for Watch out for
Chat Open-ended questions, exploration Not always the answer (NN/G); low discoverability; users want answers, not conversation (NN/G)
Inline assist Summaries, rewrite, suggestions within existing UI Must be noticeable (discoverability)
Prompt suggestions / controls Teaching capability, reducing the articulation barrier Must be relevant and specific (NN/G)
Generative UI Interfaces generated for a user's outcome, such as buttons and checkboxes inside chat Consistency and predictability (NN/G)
Agents Multi-step tasks done on the user's behalf Autonomy, consent, error recovery (NN/G)

The ✨ sparkles problem

The sparkles icon has become associated with AI but is inherently ambiguous (NN/G). Label what the feature does ("Summarise reviews"), not that it's AI.

3. Designing for non-determinism and errors

AI output varies and is sometimes wrong. Design for that explicitly.

  • Set expectations: state capabilities and limits up front ("I can answer questions about your invoices; I can't change payments").
  • Show sources and evidence: citations, links, "based on 248 reviews".
  • Make output editable: a draft the user refines, not a final answer.
  • Easy undo and recovery: especially for actions.
  • Confidence cues, where meaningful and honest.
  • Graceful failure: "I'm not sure. Here's how to find out" beats a confident error.
  • Human handoff: a path to a person when it matters. NN/G lists handoff willingness among the five qualities of trustworthy site chatbots (source).
  • Write for the web: AI output should be concise, scannable, inverted-pyramid and plain-language (NN/G).

Testing AI features

  • Test the range of outputs, not one happy path. Run the same scenario multiple times.
  • Include adversarial and edge inputs: vague requests, out-of-scope requests, sensitive topics.
  • Use interactive prototypes connected to real models (the Ramp example, Module 06).
  • Define quality rubrics with domain experts.

4. Trust, control and transparency

Durable guideline sets worth knowing

  • Microsoft's Guidelines for Human-AI Interaction (HAX): 18 guidelines grouped by initially, during interaction, when wrong and over time (for example "make clear what the system can do", "support efficient correction", "remember recent interactions", "notify users about changes"). HAX Toolkit
  • Google PAIR People + AI Guidebook: user needs, mental models, explainability and trust, feedback and control, errors and graceful failure. PAIR Guidebook

These predate the current wave but remain highly relevant, which is a good sign they're durable.

Control principles for agents

When AI acts for users (sends, books, edits, buys):

  • Preview before acting on anything consequential.
  • Explicit consent for irreversible, financial or social actions.
  • Visible activity: what it did, step by step.
  • Interruptible: users can stop it.
  • Reversible where possible.
  • Scoped permissions: what it can access, and for how long.

NN/G's lessons from studying an AI agent (Qwen): support discoverability, reuse familiar patterns, handle personal data carefully, and protect user autonomy (source).

Explanations

Different roles need different explanations of the same AI output (NN/G). An end user needs "why this result"; an admin needs "what data and rules"; compliance needs an audit trail.

Disclosure (PACED)

Revisit Module 04: Policy, Audience, Context, Expectations, Degree. NN/G notes that third-party disclosure triggered the strongest negative reactions in the studies reviewed, so proactive disclosure is often safer (source). EU transparency obligations for AI-generated content began applying in August 2026. Involve legal for regulated contexts.

5. AI agents as users of your product

A newer shift: AI agents now use digital interfaces alongside humans: browsing, filling forms, comparing products (NN/G).

Implications for design leads:

  • Accessibility helps agents too: semantic HTML, proper labels and clear structure make UIs legible to agents as well as assistive technology.
  • Clear, consistent information architecture and predictable patterns.
  • Machine-readable content: structured data, clear pricing and policies.
  • Decide your stance: which agent actions to support, require confirmation for, or block.

Common mistakes

  • AI-first, problem-second.
  • Chat as the default UI.
  • Sparkles instead of labels.
  • Testing one happy path for a non-deterministic feature.
  • Agents acting without preview or consent.
  • Opaque output with no sources, no edit and no undo.

Module 13: Leading AI adoption as a design lead

Time: about 6 hours · Output: a Design Team AI Policy, a pilot plan with metrics, a skills matrix and training plan, and a leadership pitch

Learning objectives

  • Set a strategy for AI in your design team that balances speed, quality and wellbeing.
  • Write a practical AI policy covering tools, data, research, disclosure, review and agents.
  • Select tools with clear criteria.
  • Design and run a pilot with meaningful metrics.
  • Lead change between mandates and scepticism.
  • Build team skills and a training plan.
  • Communicate value to executives with evidence.

Why this matters

NN/G's 2026 survey found that the biggest problems aren't access or buy-in. They are output quality (54%), data privacy (50%), lack of time to learn (36%), difficulty integrating into workflows (30%) and lack of training (23%). Many comments complained of mandates and unreliable output that "eat the gains" (source).

Every one of those problems is a leadership problem, which makes it your job.

1. Strategy: what are we trying to achieve?

Start with outcomes, not tools. Example strategic goals for a design team:

  1. Learn faster: test realistic prototypes with users earlier and more often.
  2. Scale quality: make design standards reach all AI-generated UI (system + context).
  3. Reduce busywork: reclaim time for research, critique and craft.
  4. Strengthen design–eng flow: fewer translation errors, faster parity.

And explicit non-goals:

  • Not to reduce research with real users.
  • Not to measure designers by output volume.
  • Not to ship prototypes as production.

One-sentence strategy template:

"We use AI to [learn faster / scale quality / reduce busywork] so that [user/business outcome], while protecting [research with real users / accessibility / craft / wellbeing]."

2. The Design Team AI Policy

Full template: templates/ai-policy.md. The essential sections:

2.1 Principles

  • Humans are accountable for everything we ship.
  • AI drafts; designers decide.
  • Real users over synthetic users for evidence.
  • Verify in proportion to risk (Module 03).
  • Accessibility is non-negotiable.
  • Be transparent about AI use where it matters (PACED).

2.2 Approved tools

A list by category (design tool AI, assistant, prototyping, coding agent), with the plan or tier, who has access, and any restrictions. NN/G: 59% of practitioners work with an approved set. That's normal and healthy.

2.4 Research rules

  • AI may assist in planning, transcription and first-pass synthesis, using approved tools and with consent.
  • Synthetic users may be used for pilot-testing materials and hypotheses, never as evidence.
  • All insights must be traceable to real evidence.

2.5 Disclosure

When and how to disclose AI use, internally (research reports, design rationale) and externally (user-facing content). Use PACED and follow legal requirements.

4. Running a pilot

Design

  • Scope: one team, one product area, 4–6 weeks.
  • Hypotheses: for example, "AI prototyping will let us run usability tests 1 sprint earlier", or "Adding DESIGN.md and AGENTS.md will cut design-QA defects on AI-built UI by 30%."
  • Baseline: measure before you start (use the past 2–3 sprints).
  • Support: training, office hours, a channel for questions.

5. Leading change: between hype and scepticism

Know your team

Archetype Typical concern What helps
Enthusiast "Let's use AI for everything!" Channel energy into experiments with metrics; pair them with sceptics
Pragmatist "Show me it works." Evidence from pilots; time-saving wins
Sceptic "It produces slop; it's unethical." Respect valid concerns; quality gates; ethics in the policy; opt-in starts
Anxious "Will this replace me?" Honest talk about role evolution; skills investment; psychological safety

Principles

  1. Invitation, not mandate. Mandates were a top complaint in NN/G's survey. Set expectations about outcomes, and let people choose tools within the policy.
  2. Protect time to learn. "Lack of time" was the #3 barrier. Budget for it, for example 2 hours a week for 8 weeks.
  3. Start with pain, not features. Ask "what's the most tedious part of your week?" and solve that first.
  4. Make learning social: demos, show-and-tell, pairing, a shared prompt library.
  5. Celebrate good judgment, including the decision not to use AI.
  6. Be honest about job anxiety. NN/G found designers and researchers felt less job-secure than PMs. Talk about the evolving role openly, and invest in people's skills.
  7. Push back upward with evidence. One respondent said: "I have to actively push against them on things I know AI doesn't do well yet." Your pilot data is how you do that credibly.

8. Ethics and sustainability

  • Bias: AI reflects its training data. Review generated content and imagery for stereotypes, and test with diverse users.
  • Labour and craft: be clear about attribution and how AI-assisted work is evaluated.
  • Environmental cost: AI use has energy costs. NN/G's survey listed ethics and sustainability as a barrier for 34%. Avoid wasteful use, such as generating 100 variants when 10 will do.
  • Accessibility: never accept AI output that regresses accessibility.

Common mistakes

  • Mandates and volume metrics.
  • No data rules, leading to an incident and then a blanket ban.
  • No time to learn.
  • Pilots without baselines.
  • Ignoring job anxiety.

Part D: Staying current without drowning

Tools change monthly, so build a sustainable learning system:

  • Weekly (30 min): skim one trusted source, such as the NN/G AI topic, Figma's release notes and help center, or your coding agent's changelog.
  • Monthly (2 h): try one new capability on a real task and log it in your AI log.
  • Quarterly (half day): review your policy, context and tool list. What's stale? What's new and proven?
  • Yearly: re-run your context experiments with the latest models. NN/G warns that "a curation decision made for today's models may be wrong for next year's."

Filter for durability. Ask of any new trend:

  1. Does it solve a real problem we have?
  2. Is it based on an open standard or a proprietary lock-in?
  3. Do practitioners report sustained use, or just launch-day excitement?
  4. Does it strengthen or weaken human judgment, users in the loop and system consistency?

If it doesn't pass at least three of the four, wait.

- `context/DESIGN.md`: visual identity and tokens
- `context/UX.md`: users, context of use, interaction standards
- `context/GLOSSARY.md`: required terminology

## 2. Design-system rules (owned by Design)

- **IMPORTANT:** Always use components from `src/components` (or `[package name]`). Never create a new Button, Input, Select, Modal, Toast, Card, etc. if one exists.
- **IMPORTANT:** If a needed component or variant does not exist, **stop and list what's missing** (name, purpose, where it's needed). Do not build ad-hoc UI.
- **Never hardcode** colours, spacing, radii, font sizes or shadows. Use tokens from `src/tokens/tokens.css` (`var(--…)`). If a token is missing, stop and ask; don't invent one.
- Match component props to Figma component properties (see Code Connect mappings).
- Implement **all states** defined in the design: empty, loading, error, disabled, focus, long content.
- **Accessibility (WCAG 2.2 AA):** labels on all inputs; visible focus on all interactive elements; logical tab order; text contrast ≥ 4.5:1; don't rely on colour alone; respect `prefers-reduced-motion`.
- **Copy:** sentence case; follow `context/GLOSSARY.md` terminology exactly; voice per `context/UX.md`.

## 3. Figma MCP flow (do not skip)

Adapted from [Figma's recommended rules](https://developers.figma.com/docs/figma-mcp-server/add-custom-rules/):

1. Run `get_design_context` for the exact node(s) linked.
2. If the response is too large, run `get_metadata` for the node map, then re-fetch only the needed nodes.
3. Run `get_screenshot` for a visual reference of the exact variant.
4. Only then implement. Download assets if needed. Don't add new icon packages; use assets from the Figma payload.
5. Treat MCP output (often React + Tailwind) as a **description of design intent, not final code style**. Translate it into this project's components and tokens.
6. Validate against the screenshot for look **and** behaviour before marking complete.

## 5. Workflow and safety

- Work on a **branch**; never commit directly to `main`.
- Show a summary of changes and the diff before committing.
- Don't add dependencies without explaining why.
- Never put secrets, keys or personal data in code, prompts or context files.
- Ask before running destructive commands.

Glossary

Plain-language definitions of every term used in the course. The module where each term is taught is shown in brackets.

Agent (AI agent). An AI system that pursues a goal over multiple steps, using tools, checking results and iterating. For example, a coding agent that reads a Figma frame, writes code, runs it and fixes errors. [02]

AGENTS.md. An open-convention markdown file at the root of a code repository with instructions for AI coding agents. Other tools use CLAUDE.md, .cursor/rules/ or .github/copilot-instructions.md. [08]

Alias (token). A token that references another token, such as color.action.primary → {color.blue.600}. Aliases enable theming. [07]

Ambient context. Raw, uncurated information streams an agent can sift through, such as Slack, email or meeting transcripts. One of NN/G's three context roles. [09]

Articulation barrier. The difficulty users have expressing their intent in words to an AI. Hybrid prompt + GUI interfaces reduce it. [12]

Auto layout. Figma's layout system for responsive, content-driven frames. Essential for systematic components. [07]

Big ball of mud. A system "scraped together for short-term functionality, not architected for long-term durability". NN/G uses the term for over-grown AI context and agent setups. [09]

Branch (git). A parallel line of work in a repository. You change things safely without affecting main. [10]

Code Connect. A Figma feature linking design components to real code components, so Dev Mode and the Figma MCP server show and use production code. Comes in UI and CLI versions. [08, 10]

Code to canvas. A Figma MCP capability that captures live UI from a browser into Figma as editable but flat layers, without a library connection. [05, 10]

Collection (Figma variables). A group of variables, often with modes. [07]

Component properties. Figma's configurable component settings: variant, boolean, text and instance swap. These should match code props. [07]

Context. Everything a model can see when it does the work: the prompt plus instructions, standards, examples and background. [02, 09]

Context engineering. Curating, structuring, scoping and maintaining the context AI uses. It matters more than prompt wording. [09]

Context library. A collection of institutional and procedural knowledge (markdown files, databases, connections) that AI agents may reference and edit. [09]

Context window. The maximum amount of content (in tokens) a model can consider at once. [02]

Contribution model. The process by which teams propose additions or changes to the design system. [11]

Design QA. Reviewing built UI against design intent, the system and accessibility standards. [10, 11]

Design system. A shared set of decisions expressed as tokens, components, patterns, documentation and governance. [07]

Design token. A named design decision stored as data, such as color.bg.surface = #FFFFFF. [07]

DESIGN.md. An open format from Google Labs (alpha, April 2026) describing a product's visual identity to coding agents. It combines YAML tokens with markdown rationale. [08]

Dev Mode. Figma's mode for developers to inspect designs. It shows Code Connect snippets when they're configured. [08]

Drift. Any difference between design-system intent and AI- or human-generated output. Tracked in the drift log. [10]

DTCG. The W3C Design Tokens Community Group. It published the first stable Design Tokens specification (2025.10), a vendor-neutral JSON format. [07]

Fidelity trap. Mistaking a polished AI prototype for production-ready work. [06]

Figma agent. Figma's built-in AI agent in Figma Design, for generating, remixing, automating busywork and giving feedback. Since May 2026 it is the entry point for features that replaced "First Draft". [05]

Figma Make. Figma's prompt-to-interactive-prototype/app tool. [05, 06]

Figma MCP server. Figma's MCP server. It lets AI agents read design context and generate code, write to the canvas, capture code to the canvas, and use Make, FigJam, Code Connect and Specs. Available as remote (recommended) or desktop. [05, 10]

Figma Specs. Figma's (open beta) feature for requirements and specs that agents can read and update via the figma-spec skill. [05]

Global context. Information that rarely changes and shapes an agent's actions across tasks, such as principles, DESIGN.md and rules. [09]

Hallucination. AI stating false information confidently. [02]

HAX guidelines. Microsoft's 18 Guidelines for Human-AI Interaction. [12]

Intent-based interaction. A UI paradigm where users specify the outcome they want rather than the commands to get there. [12]

LLM (large language model). A model trained on large amounts of text (and often other media) to predict plausible continuations. It is the basis of most generative AI tools. [02]

Local context. Information scoped to a specific task or project, such as a feature brief or Figma frame. [09]

MCP (Model Context Protocol). An open standard for connecting AI applications to tools and data sources. [02]

Mode (Figma variables). Alternate values for the same variables, such as light/dark or compact/comfortable. [07]

Mode collapse. AI outputs clustering around a narrow set of typical answers, with less variety than real humans. [02]

Multimodal. Able to process or generate several media types (text, images, audio). [02]

Non-determinism. The same input producing different outputs on different runs. [02]

PACED. NN/G's framework for deciding on AI disclosure: Policy, Audience, Context, Expectations, Degree. [04, 12]

PAIR Guidebook. Google's People + AI Research guidebook for designing AI products. [12]

Pilot. A small, time-boxed, measured trial of a new practice before scaling it. [13]

Primitive token. The raw value tier, such as blue.600. [07]

Prompt. The instruction or request given to an AI model. [03]

Pull request (PR). A proposal to merge changes from a branch, with review. [10]

RACI. Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, Informed. A framework for clarifying ownership. [11]

RAG (retrieval-augmented generation). Retrieving relevant documents and adding them to the context before generating. [02]

Rules file. Project-level instructions an agent follows every time (see AGENTS.md). [08]

Semantic token. A token that expresses intent, such as color.text.danger. Components should use semantic tokens. [07]

Skill (agent skill). Packaged instructions and resources an agent loads when relevant, often a folder with a SKILL.md. Figma provides skills such as figma-use. [09]

Sycophancy. AI's tendency to agree with and flatter the user. [02]

Synthetic users. AI-generated stand-ins for research participants. Useful for pilot-testing materials, never as evidence. [04]

Token (LLM). A chunk of text (about ¾ of an English word) that models read and write in. Not the same as a design token. [02]

UX-context design. NN/G's term for discovering and curating what an organisation knows and wants into the context that guides everything its AI tools generate. [09]

UX.md. NN/G's hypothetical file (or folder index) holding UX context: research insights, user and world models, interaction standards and glossary. [09]

Variable (Figma). Figma's implementation of design tokens. [07]

Vibe coding. Informal term for building software mainly by prompting AI, with little manual coding. [06]

World model (UX). A description of the conditions users are in while using the product (interruptions, stress, compliance, environment). [09]

Write to canvas. A Figma MCP capability (beta) letting agents create and edit real Figma content with your components and variables via the use_figma tool. [05]
